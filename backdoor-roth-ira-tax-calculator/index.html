The Backdoor Roth IRA is a two-step strategy: (1) make a non-deductible traditional IRA contribution, then (2) convert it to a Roth IRA. The tax impact depends heavily on whether you have other pre-tax IRA funds.

Step 1 — Non-Deductible Contribution: You contribute after-tax dollars to a traditional IRA. Because you exceed the income limit for a deductible contribution, you file Form 8606 to track your basis.

Step 2 — Roth Conversion: You convert the traditional IRA to Roth. Normally this is tax-free since you already paid tax. But…

The Pro-Rata Rule (The Catch): The IRS treats ALL your traditional IRA money as one pool. The taxable portion of your conversion is calculated as:

Tax-Free % = Total Non-Deductible Basis ÷ (Total IRA Balance + Conversion Amount)

Taxable Amount = Conversion Amount × (1 − Tax-Free %)

If you have $0 in other pre-tax IRAs: Your conversion is 100% tax-free — the ideal backdoor Roth scenario.

If you have pre-tax IRA money: A portion of your conversion becomes taxable income. Many people do a rollover to a 401(k) first to eliminate this problem.

Why This Matters

The Backdoor Roth IRA is one of the most powerful tax strategies available to high earners — but it's also one of the most misunderstood. In 2025, the Roth IRA income limit phases out starting at $150,000 for single filers and $236,000 for married couples filing jointly. If you're above those thresholds, the backdoor strategy is often your only path to tax-free growth.

Here's the problem most people miss: if you have any pre-tax money sitting in a traditional IRA — say, $100,000 from a rollover — and you try to convert just $7,000, the IRS won't let you cherry-pick. The pro-rata rule forces you to treat 93.5% of that conversion as taxable. On a $7,000 conversion, that's $6,545 added to your income, potentially costing $1,600–$2,600 in extra taxes at typical brackets.

Conversely, if you have zero pre-tax IRA funds, the conversion is entirely tax-free — which is why many advisors recommend rolling existing pre-tax IRA balances into a workplace 401(k) before doing the backdoor conversion. This calculator helps you model both scenarios so you can make the right call.

How It's Calculated

The IRS uses the pro-rata rule (sometimes called the "cream in the coffee" rule) to determine what portion of your conversion is taxable. Here's the exact formula:

Total IRA Value = Pre-Tax Balance + Conversion Amount
Non-Taxable % = Total Non-Deductible Basis ÷ Total IRA Value
Non-Taxable Amount = Conversion Amount × Non-Taxable %
Taxable Amount = Conversion Amount − Non-Taxable Amount
Additional Tax = Taxable Amount × Marginal Tax Rate

The marginal tax rate is estimated using 2024/2025 IRS tax brackets based on your filing status and total income (ordinary income + taxable conversion amount). The calculator uses real bracket thresholds for accurate estimates.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • The "empty IRA" strategy: Roll your pre-tax traditional IRA balance into your employer's 401(k) before December 31 of the conversion year. This eliminates the pro-rata problem entirely.
  • File Form 8606 every year: This IRS form tracks your non-deductible basis. Miss it and you could be double-taxed on the same dollars.
  • Don't wait for earnings: Convert quickly after contributing to minimize any growth in the non-Roth account — otherwise a small gain becomes taxable.
  • SEP and SIMPLE IRAs count too: Many people forget that SEP-IRA and SIMPLE IRA balances are included in the pro-rata calculation, not just traditional IRAs.
  • Married filing separately kills it: If you file MFS, your Roth income limit is only $10,000 — almost everyone is above that. Make sure your filing status is optimal before proceeding.

Frequently Asked Questions

What if I have $0 in pre-tax IRA funds?

You're in the ideal scenario. With no pre-tax IRA balance, the pro-rata rule results in 100% of your conversion being tax-free. You contributed after-tax dollars and you're converting those same after-tax dollars — no taxable income is created. This is why many high earners keep their traditional IRA balance at zero.

Does my 401(k) balance affect the pro-rata calculation?

No — 401(k), 403(b), and 457(b) balances are NOT included in the pro-rata rule. Only traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, and SIMPLE IRA accounts count. This is why rolling pre-tax IRA funds into a 401(k) (if your plan allows it) can clear the deck for a clean backdoor Roth conversion.

Can I do a backdoor Roth for my spouse too?

Yes, and it's highly recommended if you're both eligible. Each spouse has their own IRA and their own contribution limit. However, the pro-rata rule is calculated separately for each spouse based on their individual IRA balances — your spouse's pre-tax IRA does not affect your calculation.

Is the backdoor Roth IRA legal?

Yes, as of the time of writing. Congress has discussed eliminating it (notably in the 2021 Build Back Better proposal), but it remains legal. The IRS itself has acknowledged the strategy. That said, it's worth consulting a tax professional if your situation is complex, particularly if you have large existing IRA balances.
